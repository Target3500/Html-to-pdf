1. Introduction & Population vs Mendelian Genetics

Foundations

Population: A group of birds or organisms belonging to the same species, living in the same geographical area (e.g., one farm), at the same time (e.g., same season or period).

Population Genetics: The application of genetic principles to entire populations of organisms rather than single mating pairs. It studies how allele frequencies change in a whole flock over generations.

Dominance vs. Frequency: Dominance controls how an allele is expressed in an individual phenotype, NOT how common it becomes across a population. A dominant allele will not automatically take over a flock.

Feature Mendelian Genetics Population Genetics
Definition Observable traits based on simple dominant-recessive relationships. Application of genetic principles to entire populations.
Unit of Study The individual progeny (single cross). The entire population (flock).
Measurements Classifying individuals (e.g., 3:1 ratio). Needs specific mathematical frequencies.
Environment Plays no role: Phenotype = Genotype Plays a major role: Phenotype = Genotype + Environment

2. Gene, Alleles & Zygosity States

Terminology

Gene: The functional unit of genetic information in an individual located at a specific locus on a chromosome.

Alleles: Alternative molecular forms of a gene pair. Crucial rule: Alleles, not genotypes, are transmitted directly from one generation to the next.

Gene Pool: The complete set of all genetic information (all copies of all alleles) across all individuals in a population.

Zygosity Conditions:

• Homozygous: Alleles are identical (e.g., AA or aa).

• Heterozygous: Alleles are different (e.g., Aa or CB CW).

• Hemizygous: Only one allele exists for a trait (e.g., sex-linked genes on XY or ZW chromosomes).

Phenotypic Mirroring:

For classical Mendelian traits, phenotype is an exact reflection of genotype. In codominance (such as Andalusian blue chickens), heterozygotes display a distinct blended phenotype.

4. Calculating Genotypic & Allelic Frequencies

Formulas

In diploid organisms, if there are N individuals, total alleles = 2N.

Genotype Freq = (Individuals with genotype) / (Total N)
Allele Freq = (Copies of that allele) / (2N Total alleles)

• Frequency of dominant allele (p) = D + 0.5(H)

• Frequency of recessive allele (q) = R + 0.5(H)

5. The Hardy-Weinberg Law (1909)

Equilibrium Model
p + q = 1 | p^2 + 2pq + q^2 = 1

The 5 Mandatory Assumptions:

1. Very large population size
2. Random mating (panmixia)
3. No migration
4. No mutations
5. No selection

Purpose of HWE:

Acts as a Null Hypothesis to detect and measure evolutionary changes in flocks.

6. Fully Solved Practice Problems from Lecture

Step-by-Step

Problem 1: Chicken Feather Colors (500 birds)

• Data: White (BB) = 245, Blue (Bb) = 210, Black (bb) = 45.

• Allelic frequencies: p(B) = 0.7 | q(b) = 0.3 -> Population is in equilibrium.

Problem 2: PTC Tasting (1000 individuals)

• Non-tasters (tt) = 360 -> q^2 = 0.36 -> q = 0.6 -> p = 0.4.

7. Forces Altering Genetic Equilibrium

Evolutionary Factors

• Genetic Drift: Random changes in allele frequencies in small flocks.

• Selection: Differential survival or artificial breeding decisions.

• Migration: Gene flow through adding or removing birds.
